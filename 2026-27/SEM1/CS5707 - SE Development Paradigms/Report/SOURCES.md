## What each source says

### Taxonomies of business models

- **Hecker (1999).** A Netscape developer's early list of open-source business models and why a company would adopt one. Look for: the named models (support sellers, loss leader, widget frosting, accessorising, service enabler, brand licensing, sell it/free it, software franchising). The earliest taxonomy: use it as the 1999 baseline. Practitioner article in a magazine, so pair it with peer-reviewed work.
- **Krishnamurthy (2003).** Managerial overview that sorts firms into distributors, software producers (GPL and non-GPL) and third-party service providers. Look for: the classification table.
- **Watson, Boudreau, York, Greiner and Wynn (2008).** "Tracking the changing competitive conditions of the software industry." Look for: their five models (proprietary, open community, corporate distribution, sponsored OSS, second-generation OSS) and the claim that the industry moves between them. Central to the "change over time" part.
- **Fitzgerald (2006).** Open source moved from volunteer hacking to a mainstream commercial form, "OSS 2.0". Look for: how value moves from development to delivery and support.

### Selling rights to the code

- **Riehle (2012).** The single-vendor model: one firm owns the copyright, gives a version away and sells to users of the free version (dual licence or open core). Look for: why the vendor needs copyright and an engaged user community. Note Crossref's title has a typo ("open course"); cite the title as printed in the paper.
- **Bonaccorsi, Giannangeli and Rossi (2006).** Survey of 146 Italian software firms: most mix proprietary and open-source offerings, a "hybrid" model, and the paper asks whether hybrids are stable. Look for: the determinants of openness.
- **Lindman, Rossi and Puustell (2011).** Matches licence types to the business models they allow. Look for: which licences make dual licensing or open core possible.
- **Lerner and Tirole (2005).** Empirical study of what drives a project's choice of restrictive or permissive licence. Look for: how licence choice relates to commercial sponsors.

### Selling complements

- **West (2003).** How Apple, IBM and Sun mixed open and proprietary parts of their platforms. Look for: "opening parts" vs. "partly opening", and why firms open the layers they cannot profit from.
- **West and Gallagher (2006).** The paradox of firms investing in code anyone can use. Look for: four strategies (pooled R&D, spinouts, selling complements, attracting donated complements).
- **Fosfuri, Giarratana and Luzzi (2008).** Firms with patents and trademarks are more likely to release OSS products, because those rights let them profit from complements. Data: product announcements 1995–2003.

### Running service and the cloud

- **Han, Zhao, Xia and Wang (2026).** Exploratory study of Redis's licence change and whether cloud providers exploit open source. Crossref has no abstract: read the paper for the method, the Valkey fork and the findings. Your main source on the most recent shift.

### Funding without a sale

- **Overney, Meinicke, Kästner and Vasilescu (2020).** Mixed-method study of donations (PayPal, Patreon, Open Collective): how many projects ask, which receive, what the money is used for, and whether it works. Look for: the finding behind the title, "How to not get rich".
- **Shimada, Xiao, Hata, Treude and Matsumoto (2022).** GitHub Sponsors: who uses it and what it does for maintainers. Look for: how few developers earn meaningful amounts.
- **Riehle (2010).** The economic case for foundations: firms pool money into a neutral home for shared code. Look for: which firms pay and why.
- **O'Mahony and Bechky (2008).** Foundations as "boundary organisations" that let communities and firms work together. Look for: the four domains settled (governance, membership, ownership, control of production).
- **Riehle, Riemer, Kolassa and Schmidt (2014).** Share of open-source work that is paid, from commit timestamps. No abstract on Crossref: check the headline figure in the paper.

### Why give it away, and how much it is worth

- **Lerner and Tirole (2002).** Economics of open source: career signalling for individuals, and why firms take part.
- **Bonaccorsi and Rossi (2003).** Why OSS succeeds despite free-riding: coordination, network effects, firms' role.
- **Riehle (2007).** Stakeholder view: system integrators and solution providers gain most, because open source cuts their costs. Look for: who wins and who loses commercially.
- **Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou (2024).** Estimates the demand-side value of OSS to the firms that use it. SSRN working paper (HBS), not peer reviewed: cite as a working paper, use only for the headline value, and say so.
- **Wright, Nagle and Greenstein (2023).** More GitHub participation in a country leads to more new technology ventures the next year. Use it for the rise of startups built on open source.

### Cost to the community

- **Dahlander and Magnusson (2005).** Nordic firms relate to communities in symbiotic, commensalistic or parasitic ways.
- **Zhou, Mockus, Ma, Zhang and Mei (2016).** Three company-backed projects: full company control meant fewer outside newcomers but better retention.
- **Schaarschmidt, Walsh and von Kortzfleisch (2015).** How firms influence communities: a framework of governance modes, tested empirically.
- **Medappa and Srivastava (2019).** Compares projects developed by organisations with those developed by individuals and what that does to success.
