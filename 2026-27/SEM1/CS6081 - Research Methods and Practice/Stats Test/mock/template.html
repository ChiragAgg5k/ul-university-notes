The whole topic in one paragraph

A researcher runs a study to see whether something makes a difference (does sleep change how many bugs programmers write?) or whether two things go together (do people good at maths also read well?). Scores always vary a bit by chance, so a difference in the data might be real or might be luck. A statistical test works out how likely it is that the result is just chance. Which test you use depends on the design of the study (how many conditions, same or different people) and the kind of data (can it be treated as proper numbers or only as ranks?). This module only uses non-parametric tests, the ones that work on ranks.

Notes · Chapter 2

How an experiment is described

Every question describes a study. You need the vocabulary to say what is changed, what is measured and what is being tested.

The parts of an experiment

Running example from the lecture: do programmers who slept make fewer bugs than programmers who did not?

TermMeaningIn the example
HypothesisA prediction that A causes BLack of sleep causes more bugs
Independent variable (IV)What the researcher changesHours of sleep
ConditionsThe values the IV takes; each is one group of scores to compare8 hours, no sleep: 2 conditions
Dependent variable (DV)What is measured, often through a proxy because the real thing can't be measured directlyNumber of bugs (a proxy for code quality)
ControlMaking sure A, and only A, causes BSame task, same time of day, same language

Control

If something other than the IV changes between conditions, you can't tell which caused the difference. Ways to control:

  • Remove or hold constant the other factors (same task, same room, same instructions).
  • Use a control group that gets no treatment, as a baseline.
  • Make participants, or the researchers scoring them, blind to which condition they are in, so expectations don't leak into the results.

The null hypothesis

The hypothesis predicts an effect. The null hypothesis says there is no effect: no difference between conditions, or no relationship. A statistical test asks: if the null hypothesis were true, how likely would results like ours be? If very unlikely, we reject the null hypothesis, and the result supports the hypothesis.

Difference or relationship

Most studies look for a difference between conditions: the researcher sets the conditions and compares scores. Some look for a relationship (correlation) between two things measured on the same people, with nothing manipulated: maths score and reading score of each pupil. A relationship never shows cause.

Check yourself: "Does using an AI assistant change how long developers take to fix a bug?" Name the IV, the conditions and the DV.

IV: whether the developer uses an AI assistant. Conditions: with and without (2). DV: time to fix the bug.

Check yourself: what does the null hypothesis say for that study?

Using an AI assistant makes no difference to fix time.

Notes · Chapter 3

Same people or different people

The single most important design fact. It decides between Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney, and between Friedman and Kruskal-Wallis.

Related

Within subjects

The same participants do every condition. Also called related, repeated measures. Matched pairs count as related too.

The same 10 programmers are tested after 8 hours sleep and after none.

Unrelated

Between subjects

Different participants in each condition. Also called unrelated or independent groups.

6 female and 8 male programmers; bugs are compared by gender.

Strengths and weaknesses

Within subjectsBetween subjects
StrengthIndividual differences cancel out: each person is compared with themselves. Needs fewer people.No order effects: each person meets the task fresh.
WeaknessOrder effects: learning, practice, fatigue, boredom. The earlier condition affects the later one, and you can't mislead someone twice.Individual differences between the groups might explain the result. Controlled by matching or random allocation.

Order effects in a within design are usually reduced by counterbalancing: half the people do condition A first, half do B first.

When between subjects is forced

When the condition is a fixed property of the person: gender, expert or novice, nationality, which school they attend. You can't test the same person as both an expert and a novice. Also when taking part once changes the person for good (they've learned the trick, or seen through the deception).

Clues in the question
WordingDesign
"the same participants", "each developer did both", "one group under three conditions", "before and after", "rated all four designs"Within (related)
"two groups", "a different team", "separate classes", "men and women", "experts and novices"Between (unrelated)
Check yourself: patients rate their pain before and after physiotherapy. Which design?

Within subjects: before and after on the same people.

Check yourself: experts and novices read the same code. Which design, and why can't it be the other?

Notes · Chapter 4

Chance, P and significance

Half the marks on the sample paper are for explaining what a P value means. This chapter is the one to know best.

Why means are not enough

The lecture compared two experiments with almost the same means: 6.5 against 4, and 6.4 against 3.6. In the first, every score in condition 1 beats condition 2. In the second, the scores overlap a lot. Means hide variability. The second difference could easily be luck.

A statistical test weighs the size of the difference against how much the scores vary, and gives the probability that the difference is due to chance.

Descriptive and inferential

  • Descriptive statistics describe the data you have: a mean, a range, a batting average.
  • Inferential statistics go beyond it: is this difference dependable, or chance? Every test in this module is inferential.

What P means

P is the probability that a result this large is due to chance (variability in the sample) and not to the thing being tested. Strictly: the probability of getting a result at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true.

  • Small P: unlikely to be chance, so we reject the null hypothesis. The result is significant.
  • Large P: chance can't be ruled out. Not significant.
LevelPercentChanceUse
0.055%1 in 20The usual level
0.0252.5%1 in 40Seen in the lecture's Wilcoxon example
0.011%1 in 100Stricter
0.0050.5%1 in 200Seen in the Mann-Whitney example

A result is significant at a level when P is at or below it. The acceptable level depends on what is at stake: a new drug or a plane landing safely needs far more certainty than an educational aid.

Reading P values

PAt 5%?At 1%?How to say it
0.015YesNoA 1.5% probability the difference is due to chance; significant at the 5% level but not at 1%.
0.004YesYesA 0.4% probability of chance; significant at both levels.
0.13NoNoA 13% probability of chance; not significant, the null hypothesis can't be rejected.

Two warnings

  • Not significant is not "no effect". It means the evidence is too weak to rule out chance. Small samples often give this.
  • Significant is not the same as large or important. With many participants, a tiny effect can be significant.

One-tailed and two-tailed

  • A one-tailed test goes with a hypothesis that predicts a direction: "sleep-deprived programmers make more bugs."
  • A two-tailed test goes with a hypothesis that predicts a difference without saying which way: "sleep makes a difference to bugs."

You might expect a one-tailed test to be harder. It is the other way round: the two-tailed test is harder to reach significance with. Allowing either direction doubles the probability of a chance result, so the same data gives twice the P value; equivalently, the 5% is split 2.5% into each tail.

Use one-tailed only when the direction was predicted, with a reason, before the data was seen. Otherwise two-tailed is the safe default.

Sample Q3, model answer

A one-tailed test is used when the hypothesis predicts the direction of a difference; use it only if theory or earlier evidence justifies that direction in advance. A two-tailed test is used when a difference is predicted without a direction. The two-tailed test is harder to reach significance with, because allowing a difference in either direction doubles the chance probability: the 5% is split into 2.5% at each end, so the result has to be more extreme.

Check yourself: P = 0.03. Significant at 5%? At 1%? What does 0.03 mean?

Yes at 5%, no at 1%. There is a 3% probability that a result this large is due to chance.

Check yourself: a result is "not significant". Can you conclude there is no effect?

No. Chance can't be ruled out, which is different from showing there is no effect. A bigger sample might find it.

Notes · Chapter 5

Parametric or non-parametric

Parametric tests use the actual numbers. Non-parametric tests only use their order. You need to know when the numbers can't be trusted.

Three kinds of data

ScaleWhat it isExamples
NominalNamed categories, no order. You can only count how many fall in each.Programming language, gender, cancer type
OrdinalAn order, but the gaps between values aren't equal.A 1 to 7 rating, finishing position, "low / medium / high"
IntervalTrue numbers with equal gaps.Time in seconds, temperature, number of bugs

A parametric test needs all three

  1. Scores on an interval scale.
  2. A normal distribution: roughly symmetric, most scores in the middle, few at the ends.
  3. Homogeneity of variance: the scores are spread out about the same amount in each condition.

If any one fails, use a non-parametric test. In SPSS, Shapiro-Wilk checks normality and Levene's test checks equality of variance.

What non-parametric tests do

They replace scores with ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) and only ask whether scores are higher or lower, not by how much. That makes them safe for ordinal data and skewed distributions, at the cost of throwing away some information.

Clues in the question

Any of these means non-parametric: "skewed", "non-normal", "ratings on a 1 to 5 scale", "ranked", "ordinal", "a small sample", "variances differ". In the test, say which condition fails: "the data are not normally distributed, so a parametric test isn't appropriate."

Parametric equivalents

For recognition only. If a question mentions one, this is its non-parametric partner.

Notes · Chapter 6

Choosing the test

Ask four questions, always in the same order. The answers lead to exactly one test.

The four questions

  1. What is the aim? A difference between conditions, a relationship between two measures, or counts in categories?
  2. How many conditions? Two, or three or more?
  3. Same or different people? Within (related) or between (unrelated)?
  4. Was an order predicted? Only for three or more conditions. If the researcher predicts scores rise or fall across the conditions, use the trend test.

Which way is significant

Each test gives one number, the statistic, which is compared with a value from a printed table. The direction differs between tests.

TestStatisticTableSignificant when
WilcoxonWAat or below the table value
Mann-WhitneyUB1 to B4at or below
Friedmanχr² ("chi r squared")Cat or above
Page's LLDat or above
Kruskal-WallisHEat or above
JonckheereSGat or above
Spearmanrhofar from 0, with enough people
Chi-squareχ²at or above

Memory hook: the two-condition tests take the smaller rank total, so small is significant. Everything else measures how big the differences are, so big is significant.

Sample Q5, model answer

One group of radiologists does all three conditions, so the design is related (within subjects). There are three conditions, and the data are non-normal, so a non-parametric test for three or more related conditions is needed: Friedman, which tests whether the conditions differ at all. Because the conditions have a natural order (slept, one night without, two nights without) and errors would be predicted to rise, Page's L trend test is the stronger choice if that order is stated in the hypothesis.

Notes · Chapter 7

Ranking

Every non-parametric test except chi-square starts by turning scores into ranks. How you rank depends on the design.

The rules

  • The smallest score gets rank 1.
  • Tied scores share the average of the ranks they occupy.
  • Check: the ranks of N scores always add up to N × (N + 1) ÷ 2.
Score4833174
Rank4.572.52.5164.5

Sorted, the scores are 1, 3, 3, 4, 4, 7, 8. The two 3s take positions 2 and 3, so each gets 2.5. The two 4s take 4 and 5, so each gets 4.5. Total 28 = 7 × 8 ÷ 2.

Three ways to rank

DesignHow to rankTests
Different peopleRank all scores from every condition together as one list, then add up the ranks in each condition.Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis
Same people, 2 conditionsEach row is one person. Take the difference (condition 1 minus condition 2), rank the differences by size ignoring the sign, then put the signs back. A difference of zero is dropped and N falls by one.Wilcoxon
Same people, 3+ conditionsRank across each person's own row (1, 2, 3), then add up each column.Friedman, Page's L
Two measuresRank each measure separately, then compare each person's two ranks.Spearman

Always look at the means first to see which way the data goes.

Check yourself: rank 10, 20, 20, 20, 30.

1, 3, 3, 3, 5. The three 20s take positions 2, 3 and 4, average 3.

Notes · Chapter 8

Two conditions: Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney

Same people: Wilcoxon. Different people: Mann-Whitney. For both, a small statistic is significant.

Same people

Wilcoxon (signed-rank)

  1. For each person, difference = condition 1 − condition 2.
  2. Drop zeros. N is the number left.
  3. Rank the differences by size, ignoring the sign. Ties share the average.
  4. Add up the ranks of the positive differences and of the negative ones separately.
  5. W is the smaller sum. Compare with Table A for that N: significant if W is at or below the table value.

Why small? If nearly every difference goes the same way, the sum for the other direction is tiny.

Worked example from the lecture

Bugs by 10 programmers with 8 hours sleep (C1) and with none (C2).

Programmer12345678910
C1 − C2−2−1+1−4+1−3−20−4−2
Signed rank−5−2+2−8.5+2−7−5drop−8.5−5
  • Sizes 1, 1, 1 take ranks 1–3 → 2 each. Sizes 2, 2, 2 take 4–6 → 5 each. Size 3 → 7. Sizes 4, 4 take 8–9 → 8.5 each.
  • Positive sum 2 + 2 = 4. Negative sum 41. W = 4, N = 9.
  • One-tailed, Table A gives 6 at P = 0.025. 4 is below 6, so significant at P = 0.025, a 1 in 40 chance.
Different people

Mann-Whitney (U)

  1. Rank all scores from both groups together.
  2. Add up the ranks in each group: T1 and T2.
  3. Work out U and U′, and take the smaller.
  4. Compare with Tables B1 to B4 (one table per significance level): significant if U is at or below the table value.
U = n1·n2 + n1(n1 + 1) ÷ 2 − T1   U′ = n1·n2 − U
Worked example from the lecture

Bugs by gender: n1 = 6, n2 = 8. Rank totals T1 = 24.5, T2 = 80.5.

  • U = 6 × 8 + 6 × 7 ÷ 2 − 24.5 = 48 + 21 − 24.5 = 44.5
  • U′ = 48 − 44.5 = 3.5, the smaller.
  • 3.5 is below the table value of 4, so significant at P = 0.005.
Sample Q1, model answer

Choosing Wilcoxon means the study compared two conditions in a related design: the same participants did both (or matched pairs). It is non-parametric, so the data were ordinal or not normally distributed. P = 0.015 means there is a 1.5% probability that a difference this large is due to chance. That is below 0.05, so the difference is significant at the 5% level and the null hypothesis is rejected; it is not significant at the stricter 1% level.

Check yourself: a Mann-Whitney result. What does that tell you about the design?

Two conditions, different people in each, and data treated as non-parametric.

Notes · Chapter 9

Three or more conditions

Friedman and Kruskal-Wallis say whether the conditions differ, but not how. When an order is predicted, the trend tests Page's L and Jonckheere say more.

Same people

Friedman (χr²)

Rank across each person's row, add up each condition's ranks. The more the totals differ, the less likely it is chance.

χr² = [12 ÷ (N·C·(C + 1))] × ΣT² − 3·N·(C + 1)

N = people, C = conditions, T = each condition's rank total.

Lecture example: 8 programmers with 8, 4 and 6 hours sleep; rank totals 11, 21, 16.

(12 ÷ 96) × (121 + 441 + 256) − 96 = 102.25 − 96 = 6.25

Limit: no direction. A significant Friedman says the conditions differ, not which one or in what order. Pairwise Wilcoxon tests can locate the difference.

Same people, order predicted

Page's L

Same ranking as Friedman. Put the conditions in the predicted order, then weight their totals 1, 2, 3… Later columns count for more, so a trend in the predicted direction gives a large L.

L = T1 × 1 + T2 × 2 + T3 × 3 …

Lecture example: ordered 8 hours, 6 hours, 4 hours, with totals 11, 16, 21: L = 11 + 32 + 63 = 106. Against Table D this was not significant.

Different people

Kruskal-Wallis (H)

Rank all scores together, add up each group's ranks. Groups can be different sizes.

H = [12 ÷ (N(N + 1))] × Σ(T² ÷ n) − 3(N + 1)

Lecture example: three groups of 4, 3 and 4 (N = 11); rank totals 38, 14, 14. H = (12 ÷ 132) × (361 + 65.33 + 49) − 36 = 7.21.

Limit: no direction, like Friedman. Pairwise Mann-Whitney tests can locate the difference.

Different people, order predicted

Jonckheere (S)

No ranking. Put the groups in the predicted order. For each score, count how many scores in the groups to its right are larger. S is built from those counts; large is significant.

Needs the same number of people in every group. With unequal groups, fall back to Kruskal-Wallis.

How to spot the trend version

The question says scores are predicted to rise or fall across conditions that have a natural order: 0, 1, 2 nights without sleep; 0, 5, 10 mg; novice, intermediate, expert. "Do they differ?" without a predicted order means Friedman or Kruskal-Wallis.

Check yourself: three separate groups of 8 get 0, 2 or 4 hours training; scores are predicted to rise. Which test?

Jonckheere: different people, three conditions, predicted order, equal groups.

Check yourself: a significant Kruskal-Wallis result. Can you say which group scored highest?

No. It only shows the groups differ. Look at the rank totals descriptively, or run pairwise Mann-Whitney tests.

Notes · Chapter 10

Relationships and counts

Spearman measures whether two things go together. Chi-square compares counts in categories. Both appeared on the sample paper.

Spearman's rho

Used when you look for an association between two variables measured on the same people, not a difference between conditions. Nothing is manipulated, so it can't show cause and effect.

A Spearman result has two separate parts:

  • The coefficient, rho, from −1 to +1. The sign gives the direction (positive: both rise together; negative: one rises as the other falls). The size gives the strength: near 0 is no relationship, near ±1 is very strong. As a rough guide, below 0.3 is weak, 0.3 to 0.7 moderate, above 0.7 strong.
  • The P value: whether a correlation that strong could be chance. This depends heavily on how many people there are.
rho = 1 − (6 × Σd²) ÷ (N(N² − 1))

Rank each variable separately; d is each person's difference between their two ranks. A small Σd² means the rankings agree.

Lecture example: maths and reading for 12 pupils, Σd² = 61. rho = 1 − 366 ÷ 1716 = 0.79, a strong positive correlation.

The traps

ResultReading
Strong rho, high P (0.95, P = 0.13)Not significant despite the strength. Usually too few people. Can't claim a relationship.
Weak rho, low P (0.31, P = 0.002)Significant but still weak. Usually many people. Real but small.
Any correlationCorrelation is not causation.
Sample Q2, model answer

rho = 0.95 is a very strong positive correlation: GPs who rank fast at diagnosis also rank fast at the crossword (rho runs from −1 to +1). But P = 0.13 is above 0.05, so the correlation is not significant: there is a 13% probability of a correlation this strong arising by chance. A very strong coefficient with a weak P points to a very small sample. We can't reject the null hypothesis or claim a relationship; the study should be repeated with more GPs. Even a significant result would only show an association, not that one skill causes the other.

Chi-square (χ²)

Used only when people or things are allocated to categories and counted, not given scores. Each one sits in exactly one cell of a table.

  • The researcher can't decide in advance how many land in each cell; that's the result.
  • Usually more than 20 subjects, and at least 5 in each cell, before it can be trusted.

The test compares the observed count in each cell with the count expected if the two variables were unrelated.

Expected = row total × column total ÷ grand total
χ² = Σ (Observed − Expected)² ÷ Expected   df = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1)

The bigger the gaps between observed and expected, the bigger χ², and the less likely the pattern is chance. Compare with the critical value for those degrees of freedom: 5.99 for df = 2, 9.49 for df = 4 (at 5%).

Worked example from the lecture
Bottom-upTop-downMixedTotal
Experts6152344

  • Full χ² = 3.11 with df = (2 − 1)(3 − 1) = 2. The 5% critical value is 5.99, so not significant.
Sample Q4, model answer

The test is chi-square: the data are counts of tools falling into categories (tool type and cancer category), each tool counted once. Bonus: it compares each observed count with the count expected if tool type and cancer category were unrelated. For LNC and tool type 1, expected = 72 × 36 ÷ 144 = 18, against 25 observed; that cell contributes (25 − 18)² ÷ 18 ≈ 2.72. Adding all nine cells gives χ² (about 10.19), compared with 9.49 for df = 4 at 5%: significant.

Chi-square: projects are allocated to categories and counted.

Notes · Chapter 11

Writing full-mark answers

Each question is worth 4 marks. Aim for four distinct points; the marker is looking for each one.

"What does this result mean?"

  1. Design the test implies: number of conditions, same or different people, non-parametric data, trend or not.
  2. The statistic: for a correlation, its direction and strength.
  3. P: the probability that a result this large is due to chance, with the number in words ("a 1.5% probability").
  4. Conclusion: compare with 0.05 and 0.01, say whether the null hypothesis is rejected, add a caution.

"Which test should be used?"

  1. Same or different people, with the evidence from the question.
  2. How many conditions (or that it is a relationship, or counts).
  3. Why non-parametric (skewed, ordinal, non-normal).
  4. The test, plus the trend version if an order is predicted, or the follow-up if direction matters.

Cautions worth a mark

  • Not significant means chance can't be ruled out, not that there is no effect.
  • Significant doesn't mean large: big samples make small effects significant.
  • A strong correlation with a high P usually means too few people.
  • Friedman and Kruskal-Wallis don't say which conditions differ.
  • Correlation is not causation.

Phrases to reuse

  • "There is a 3% probability that a difference this large is due to chance."
  • "P is below 0.05, so the result is significant at the 5% level and the null hypothesis is rejected."
  • "It is not significant at the stricter 1% level."
  • "The same participants took part in every condition, so the design is related."
  • "The data are not normally distributed, so a non-parametric test is needed."
